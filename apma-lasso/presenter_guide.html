Section 3 run-through: complexity control with the lasso

Read the first box. Then go slide by slide: what it means in plain English, what each symbol is, the reasoning one step at a time, and the words to say.

Start here: the whole idea with no math

  1. We have a formula that predicts an artwork's price per square centimeter from 24 traits (size, medium, how famous the artist is, and so on). Each trait has one number attached that says how much it moves the price. Those 24 numbers are the coefficients.
  2. The worry: a formula with many numbers to tune can end up memorizing quirks of the past sales instead of real patterns. It then looks great on old sales and predicts new ones badly. That is called overfitting.
  3. The test: charge the formula a fee for every coefficient it uses. A trait that is not pulling its weight is no longer worth the fee, so its number gets set to zero and the trait drops out. This fee method is the lasso. The size of the fee is called eta.
  4. How big should the fee be? Try many sizes. For each one, see how well the formula predicts sales it was never shown. Keep the fee that predicts best. This is cross-validation.
  5. What happened: the best fee was almost zero. Every one of the 24 traits earns its place. So Jana's model was not overfitting.

Every technical word

The number attached to a trait. Example: 0.837 for established artists means their work sells for more.

Ordinary least squares. Jana's method: pick the coefficients that make the prediction errors as small as possible. No fee.

The fee. An extra cost added for using large coefficients.

The fee is based on the plain size of each coefficient. It can set coefficients to exactly zero.

A different fee, based on the square of each coefficient. It shrinks coefficients but never makes them zero.

A formula you can just plug numbers into to get the answer. OLS and ridge have one. The lasso does not.

What the lasso is

70 sec

In plain English

The formula at the top is a score we want to make as low as possible. It is two things added together: how wrong the predictions are, plus a fee for using big coefficients.

Symbols on this slide

min over β

½‖y − Xβ‖²
how wrong the predictions are: errors squared and added up (the ½ is only for tidiness)
η
the size of the fee
Σ from j = 1 to d
add up over all 24 coefficients
|β j|
the size of coefficient j
p
which kind of fee: 1 or 2
β̂ = (XᵀX + 2ηI)⁻¹Xᵀy
the ready-made answer for ridge. You only need to say that a formula exists.

The reasoning, step by step

  1. Jana's method makes only the first part small: the prediction errors.
  2. We add a second part: a fee for every bit of coefficient used.
  3. If eta is zero, the fee is free, and we are back to Jana's answer.
  4. If eta is large, it becomes cheaper to drop weak traits than to pay for them.
  5. There are two common fees. Ridge charges for the square of each coefficient. The lasso charges for its plain size.
  6. Ridge has a ready-made formula. Nothing to work out.
  7. The lasso does not. The reason: the graph of |x| is a V. At the tip of the V there is no single slope, so the usual calculus trick (set the slope to zero and solve) breaks down exactly there.
  8. So for the lasso we need a step-by-step procedure. The upside: it sets some coefficients to exactly zero, which tells us which traits matter.

Say this

We keep Jana's sum of squared errors and add a cost: eta times the sum of the sizes of the coefficients. Eta is a dial. At zero we get OLS. Turning it up pushes coefficients toward zero. With p equal to 2, called ridge, there is still a formula. With p equal to 1, the lasso, the absolute value has a corner at zero, so there is no formula and we need an algorithm. In return, it sets some coefficients exactly to zero. My derivation starts from the sum of squares, ends at an update rule, and the tool is solving for one coefficient at a time.

If asked

Why the lasso and not ridge?

Ridge has a ready-made formula, so there is nothing to derive. The lasso needs an algorithm, and it also tells you which traits to keep.

What is eta?

The size of the fee on coefficients. Zero means no fee, which is ordinary least squares.

Derivation, part 1

50 sec

In plain English

We cannot solve for all 24 numbers at once. So we pretend 23 of them are already known and ask: what is the best value for the one that is left? That is a much easier question.

Symbols on this slide

G = XᵀX
table of how much each pair of traits overlaps
c = Xᵀy
how strongly each trait goes along with price
G jj
trait j's overlap with itself: just a positive number
G jk
overlap between trait j and another trait k
f(β j)
the score, looked at as depending only on coefficient j
ρ j
leftover usefulness of trait j
Σ over k ≠ j
add up over all the other traits
const
everything that does not involve coefficient j, so it cannot change the answer

The reasoning, step by step

  1. Standardize: put all traits on the same scale, so the fee treats them equally.
  2. Expand: multiply out the squared error. It is the same kind of algebra as (a − b)² = a² − 2ab + b². The result can be written using the two tables G and c.
  3. Freeze 23 coefficients. Keep only the terms that contain the one we are solving for.
  4. What is left has three pieces. A bowl shape (the β² term). A tilt that slides the bottom of the bowl left or right (the ρ term). And the V-shaped fee.
  5. Rho is the tilt. It starts from how strongly trait j goes with price (c j), then subtracts what the other traits already explain. What remains is trait j's own contribution.

Say this

First I standardize the columns, so one eta is fair to every feature. Then I expand the sum of squares, the same step Jana did. G and c are the two pieces of her normal equation. Now freeze every coefficient except one. What is left is a parabola in beta j, minus rho j times beta j, plus eta times its absolute value. Rho j is the pull on that coefficient: how much feature j still explains after the other features have done their share.

If asked

Why standardize?

So the fee is fair. Otherwise a trait measured in large units would have a small coefficient and escape the fee.

What is rho?

How much trait j still explains about price after the other 23 traits have explained what they can.

Derivation, part 2

80 sec

In plain English

Find the lowest point of "bowl plus V". The answer is simple: take the trait's leftover usefulness (rho) and knock off eta. If nothing is left, the coefficient is zero.

Symbols on this slide

f′
the slope of the score
= 0
at the lowest point of a smooth curve, the slope is zero
⇒
therefore
S(ρ, η)
the rule: knock eta off rho; if that would cross zero, stop at zero
sign(ρ)
keeps the original plus or minus
max(|ρ| − η, 0)
rho's size minus eta, but never below zero
←
set the coefficient to this value

The reasoning, step by step

  1. The lowest point of a smooth curve is where its slope is zero.
  2. To the right of zero, the V is just a straight line going up at steepness eta. Add that to the bowl's slope, set the total to zero, and solve: coefficient = (rho − eta) / G. This only makes sense if the answer is positive, which needs rho bigger than eta.
  3. To the left of zero it is the mirror image: coefficient = (rho + eta) / G. This needs rho below minus eta.
  4. If rho is small, between minus eta and eta, neither answer lands on its own side. The curve goes downhill toward zero from both directions. So the lowest point is the tip of the V: exactly zero.
  5. All three cases fit in one rule, called the soft-threshold, in the box.
  6. Example with eta = 1: rho of 3 gives 2. Rho of −2.5 gives −1.5. Rho of 0.4 gives 0.
  7. Check: with eta = 0 nothing is knocked off, and the method gives ordinary least squares.

Say this

Now minimize that one-variable function, by cases. If beta j is positive, I can differentiate and solve: rho minus eta, over G j j. If it is negative, the same steps give rho plus eta. If rho is between minus eta and eta, neither case works, and the minimum sits on the corner: exactly zero. One line covers all three. It is called the soft-threshold. The plot shows it: flat at zero inside the band, and shifted toward zero outside it. And with eta equal to zero, this is just iterating the normal equation, so we get OLS back.

If asked

Why does the lasso give exact zeros?

Because of the corner in the V. The fee pushes just as hard near zero as far from it, so a weak trait gets pushed all the way to zero. Ridge's fee fades out near zero, so it never quite gets there.

What does the plot show?

The dashed line is no fee: the coefficient equals rho. The solid line is with the fee: zero in the shaded band, and otherwise pulled toward zero by eta.

Pseudocode 1: the lasso recipe

50 sec

In plain English

This is the recipe the computer follows. Start with every coefficient at zero. Fix them one at a time using the rule from the last slide. Keep going around until they stop changing.

Symbols on this slide

The reasoning, step by step

  1. Build the two tables G and c from the data, once.
  2. Set all 24 coefficients to zero.
  3. For coefficient 1: work out its rho, apply the soft-threshold rule, store the new value.
  4. Do the same for coefficient 2, then 3, up to 24.
  5. Go around again. You have to, because changing one coefficient changes rho for the others.
  6. Stop when a full trip around changes nothing.

Say this

Here is the algorithm. Compute G and c once. Start every coefficient at zero. Then, for each coefficient, compute its pull and apply the soft-threshold. Go around again until nothing changes. The two lines in the middle are exactly the derivation. I checked the code two ways: with eta equal to zero it reproduces Jana's OLS, and with eta above zero it agrees with scikit-learn's lasso to 8 decimal places.

If asked

How do you know the code is right?

Two checks. With no fee it gives the same coefficients as Jana's OLS. With a fee it gives the same answer as scikit-learn, a standard Python library.

Does it always finish?

Yes for this problem. There is a published proof by Tseng from 2001. I am citing it, not proving it.

Pseudocode 2: choosing the fee

70 sec

In plain English

Like practice exams. You do not test yourself on questions you already saw the answers to. We hide some sales, fit on the rest, and grade the predictions on the hidden ones. We do that for every fee size and keep the best.

Symbols on this slide

K

η max

argmin

η*
the winner
SE
standard error: how uncertain the error estimate is
η 1se
the biggest fee that is within one SE of the winner

The reasoning, step by step

  1. Split the artists into 5 groups. All of one artist's sales stay together.
  2. Hide group 1. For each fee size, fit the lasso on groups 2 to 5 and measure the error on group 1.
  3. Repeat, hiding group 2, then 3, 4 and 5.
  4. Average the 5 errors for each fee size.
  5. The fee with the lowest average error wins. That is eta star.
  6. Also note eta 1se: the largest fee that does about as well. It gives the simplest model that is still as good as the best.

Say this

That gives a different model for every eta, so a second algorithm picks one. Split the artists into five groups. Hide one group, fit on the other four for every eta on a grid, and measure the error on the hidden group. Rotate so each group is hidden once, and average. Eta star is the one with the lowest held-out error. Eta one-s-e is the strongest penalty whose error is within one standard error of that. The groups are by artist, not by sale, because one artist's sales look alike.

If asked

Why not pick the fee that fits the data best?

On data the model has already seen, no fee always fits best. Only hidden data shows whether the model learned a pattern or memorized.

Why split by artist?

One artist's sales look alike. If the same artist were in both the fitting data and the hidden data, the test would be too easy.

If asked

So the lasso did nothing?

It answered the question. It tested whether the model was too complex, and the answer is no. Before this, that was only assumed.

Why was (ln A)² dropped when Jana says it matters a lot?

It moves together with ln A, so the lasso keeps one of the two. It does not mean size has a straight-line effect.

General questions and plain answers

What is the one-sentence summary?

I tested whether our 24-coefficient model was too complex by adding a penalty and checking predictions on hidden sales, and it was not.

Did you get better coefficients?

No. The best penalty was almost zero, so the coefficients are essentially Jana's. That tells us hers were already right.

What makes the lasso hard?

The absolute value has a corner at zero, so you cannot solve it with one formula. You have to solve for one coefficient at a time and repeat.

What is coordinate descent?

Solving for one coefficient at a time while holding the others fixed, and cycling until nothing changes.

What is the soft-threshold?

The rule for one coefficient: take its leftover usefulness, subtract eta, and if nothing is left, set it to zero.

When would the lasso matter more?

With many more traits than we have, for example one for each of the 8,372 artists. Then OLS would overfit and the penalty would help.

Something you do not know

"I don't know. I would check it in the notebook." That is a fine answer. Do not guess.
